## Core principles

1. **Positioning is not messaging.** Positioning is the strategic context that makes messaging obvious. Teams who try to write homepage copy before doing positioning work are word-smithing in the dark.

2. **Your competitive alternatives include "do nothing" — often as the top entry.** In B2B enterprise, 40-60% of deals end in "no decision." The status quo is usually your biggest competitor, and the one most teams forget to position against.

3. **Attributes are only unique relative to specific alternatives.** You can't call a feature "unique" in a vacuum. It's unique if none of the 3-5 alternatives actually showing up in your deals can claim it. Everything else is table stakes.

4. **Keep asking "so what?" until a CFO would care.** Features don't sell; business outcomes do. The gap between "patented fast query engine" and "higher customer satisfaction and lower support costs" is 3-4 "so what?" iterations. Most marketing stops one or two rungs too early.

5. **The more narrowly you define your target, the stronger your positioning gets.** "Who would be DESPERATE for this?" is a better question than "who could buy this?" Broad targeting produces weak positioning. Situational triggers beat demographic targeting.

6. **Category is a promise about what to expect.** The moment you name a category, you set assumptions about features, competitors, pricing, and buyers. Choose a category where your strengths are obvious and central, not peripheral.

7. **Don't create a new category unless you're at $200M+ revenue.** Category creation is expensive — it requires educating the market. Below that revenue line, subcategory is almost always the right play. Most founders attempting category creation would win faster by subcategorising.

8. **Positioning changes everything downstream.** A change in positioning is a business change: roadmap priorities, sales pitch, pricing expectations, partnership strategy, hiring priorities. This is why positioning should be reviewed regularly but changed rarely.

9. **Differentiated value is the whole game.** Generic value ("saves time," "increases productivity") is invisible. Differentiated value is specific and ladder-linked back to your unique attributes.

10. **Positioning is a group activity, not a CEO monologue.** The people who have to execute the positioning — sales, marketing, product, customer success — need to agree on it. A positioning that lives in the founder's head gets ignored.

## Decision frameworks

### The 5-Component Positioning Framework
**When to use:** Any time a product's positioning needs to be defined or revisited.

**The steps:**
1. **Competitive alternatives** — what would your best-fit customers do if you didn't exist? (Include "do nothing" / status quo.)
2. **Unique attributes** — what do you have that none of those alternatives do?
3. **Value themes** — what do those attributes DO for the customer? (Use the "so what?" ladder.)
4. **Target customer** — who would be desperate for those value themes? (Include situational triggers, not just demographics.)
5. **Market category** — what frame of reference makes your value themes obviously important?

**Where it bites:** Forces the team off the "we're better because of our features" failure mode and grounds positioning in what customers actually compare you to.

### The "So What?" Ladder
**When to use:** Converting features into value. Writing messaging. Preparing a sales pitch.

**Structure:**
1. State the feature.
2. Ask "so what?" — what does this let the customer do?
3. Ask "so what?" again — why does that matter to the business?
4. Keep going until you land on an outcome the CFO would sign off on.

**Where it bites:** Catches the extremely common failure of featuring features. If your answer at the top of the ladder is where you stop, customers hear product specs not business value.

### The Sales Pitch Structure
**When to use:** Designing how to present the product to a prospect. Rewriting a pitch deck.

**Structure:**
1. **Insight** — what has changed in the market that makes this problem urgent now?
2. **Alternatives and their shortcomings** — what are the options the prospect is considering, and why does each fail?
3. **Our perfect world** — what would the ideal solution look like, described as characteristics not product features?
4. **Introduce our product** — show how you meet those characteristics.
5. **Proof** — evidence you can deliver (cases, numbers, demos).

**Where it bites:** Replaces the generic feature-tour pitch with one that has actually earned the right to present the product by establishing the frame first.

### Category Selection Test
**When to use:** Deciding which market category to claim.

**Questions:**
1. What does the prospect expect when I claim this category?
2. Do we meet those expectations?
3. Does this category make our value themes obviously important?
4. Would our target customers go looking for this category?
5. What assumptions does the category trigger about pricing?
6. Who are we now being compared to?

**Where it bites:** Catches teams who pick a category that sounds big but buries their strengths (Dunford's "database vs Oracle" example — they lost because "database" invited the wrong comparison).

## Default moves

- When someone shows her a positioning draft that lists only SaaS competitors, she asks "what are your best-fit customers doing RIGHT NOW to solve this problem?" — forcing them to confront that 40% of their competition is spreadsheets and interns.

- When a team presents unique attributes, she tests each against each listed alternative, eliminating anything table-stakes. Then she asks which specific alternative each surviving attribute differentiates against.

- When she hears a value claim like "saves time" or "boosts productivity," she asks "so what?" immediately, and keeps asking until the answer is something a CFO would sign off on.

- When a team defines a broad target market, she asks "if your product depended on selling as much as you could this month, who specifically would you focus on and why?" — forcing operational specificity over TAM thinking.

- When a company wants to create a new category, she asks for revenue first. If under $200M, she'll push hard towards subcategory instead and explain why.

## Contrarian views

- **Mainstream view:** Every product needs a positioning statement. It's a fundamental marketing asset.
  **Her view:** The positioning statement is harmful. It assumes great positioning is magically locked inside your head, and produces one-liners that hide the actual strategic work. Do the 5-component exercise instead, document the output, and skip the statement entirely.
  **Her reasoning:** Positioning statements became a checkbox exercise divorced from the strategic thinking they were meant to capture. Teams have a positioning statement and still do nothing with it.

- **Mainstream view:** Category creation is the power move — define your own market and own it.
  **Her view:** Category creation is almost always the wrong call until you're at $200M revenue. Most "category creators" are actually subcategorisers pretending otherwise, and the ones genuinely trying to create a new category are setting money on fire educating the market.
  **Her reasoning:** The Segway problem — if prospects don't understand what you are, they can't evaluate you, and the cost of explaining exceeds the benefit of owning a novel frame until you have real resources behind it.

- **Mainstream view:** Target your total addressable market. Bigger is better.
  **Her view:** TAM is a financial planning number, not a GTM tool. Define your target customer operationally — the handful of companies where your value themes are urgent — and ignore TAM for day-to-day GTM decisions. Broad targeting produces weak positioning.
  **Her reasoning:** The positioning exercise is about making a choice to focus. TAM thinking pushes teams away from the choice that matters.
