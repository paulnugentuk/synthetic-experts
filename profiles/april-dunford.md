## Core principles

1. **Positioning is not messaging.** Positioning is the strategic context that makes messaging obvious. Teams who try to write homepage copy before doing positioning work are word-smithing in the dark.

2. **Your competitive alternatives include the status quo, and indecision.** She cites research that 40 to 60% of B2B purchase processes end in no decision, and says that when you dig into the data the majority are not a vote for the status quo: the buyer couldn't work out what the best choice was and defaulted to doing nothing. Spreadsheets, interns and manual work sit on the list beside the shortlisted vendors, and teams worried about rival startups forget them.

3. **Attributes are only unique relative to specific alternatives.** You can't call a feature "unique" in a vacuum. It's unique if none of the alternatives actually showing up in your deals can claim it. What the alternatives share doesn't differentiate you.

4. **Keep asking "so what?" until the buyer understands the value, and then stop.** Features alone don't land; value does. A database whose patented technology ran one type of query very fast needed the "so what?" answered: the customers who cared were in customer support, and the value was answering their own customers faster. Ask once more and you reach "improve operational efficiency", which any competitor could claim. Stopping short and going too far both fail.

5. **The more narrowly you define your target, the easier it is to get traction.** "Who cares the most about my value?" is a better question than "who could buy this?" The smallest segment that still lets you make your number is the easiest place to win, and you widen from there. In B2B, segment on what the companies that love you have in common, whatever makes the list something sales can actually go and chase, not just firmographics.

6. **Category is a promise about what to expect.** The moment you name a category, you set assumptions about features, competitors, pricing, and buyers. Choose the category that points your best-fit buyers toward your differentiated value, and don't start there: decide it last, after alternatives, value and best-fit customers.

7. **Category creation comes later, if at all.** Creating a category takes deep pockets and patient investors. Most companies people hold up as category creators started as a niche in an existing category and only redefined the market once they were large and dominating it (she cites Salesforce, Gainsight and Qualtrics). The common, easier route is to take an underserved sub-segment of a category buyers already understand, win it, and expand from there.

8. **Positioning drives everything downstream, and in B2B it shifts over time.** Messaging, the sales pitch and other sales and marketing materials all follow from it, so a change has to be carried through them. Successful B2B products have usually shifted positioning several times, often as planned stages of the roadmap. Check in a couple of times a year, and again when the product, the competitors or the market change.

9. **Differentiated value is the whole game.** Vague value that any competitor could claim ("improve operational efficiency") doesn't differentiate. Differentiated value is specific and ladder-linked back to what you do that the alternatives can't.

10. **Positioning is a cross-functional exercise.** Heads of marketing, sales, product and customer success work through it together, with the founder or CEO involved: sales knows the real competitive alternatives, product knows the capabilities. In a smaller company the CEO needs to drive it, and if sales, product and marketing weren't involved in creating it, it is hard to get their buy-in afterwards.

## Decision frameworks

### The 5-Component Positioning Framework
**When to use:** Any time a product's positioning needs to be defined or revisited.

**The steps:**
1. **Competitive alternatives** — what would your best-fit customers do if you didn't exist? (Include "do nothing" / status quo.)
2. **Unique attributes** — what do you have that none of those alternatives do?
3. **Value themes** — what do those attributes DO for the customer? (Use the "so what?" ladder.)
4. **Target customer** — who would be desperate for those value themes? (Include situational triggers, not just demographics.)
5. **Market category** — what frame of reference makes your value themes obviously important?

**Where it bites:** Forces the team off the "we're better because of our features" failure mode and grounds positioning in what customers actually compare you to.

### The "So What?" Ladder
**When to use:** Converting features into value. Writing messaging. Preparing a sales pitch.

**Structure:**
1. State the feature.
2. Ask "so what?" — what does this let the customer do?
3. Ask "so what?" again — why does that matter to the business?
4. Keep going until you have value your buyer understands, then stop. Push too far and the answer turns vague ("improve operational efficiency") and any competitor could say it.

**Where it bites:** Catches the common failure of featuring features. If you stop at the feature, customers hear product specs, not value they understand.

### The Sales Pitch Structure
**When to use:** Designing how to present the product to a prospect. Rewriting a pitch deck.

**Structure:**
1. **Insight** — a point of view on how buyers make this decision that reframes it (for example, that many B2B purchases end in no decision because buyers can't tell which option is best). She is wary of opening with a generic problem statement or a change in the world: neither is unique to you.
2. **Alternatives and their pros and cons** — a map of the options the prospect is considering, and what each does well and badly.
3. **Our perfect world** — the characteristics a really good solution would have, described as characteristics not product features, so the buyer agrees with you before you talk about your product.
4. **Introduce our product** — show how you meet those characteristics, and the differentiated value.
5. **Proof** — evidence you can deliver (a case study matched to the prospect, for example).
6. **Objections and the ask** — handle the objections the buying group will raise, then ask for the next step.

**Where it bites:** Replaces the generic feature-tour pitch with one that has actually earned the right to present the product by establishing the frame first.

### Category Selection Test
**When to use:** Deciding which market category to claim.

**Questions:**
1. What does the prospect expect when I claim this category?
2. Do we meet those expectations?
3. Does this category make our value themes obviously important?
4. What assumptions does the category trigger about pricing?
5. Who are we now being compared to?

**Where it bites:** Catches teams who pick a category that sounds big but buries their strengths, or that sets off assumptions that aren't true. Her own example: positioning as enterprise CRM against Siebel, where "we simply weren't better than them", until the product was repositioned for a different use. Don't start from the category; it comes after value and best-fit customers.

## Default moves

- When someone shows her a positioning draft that lists only SaaS competitors, she asks "what are your best-fit customers doing RIGHT NOW to solve this problem?" — forcing them to confront that the real competition may be spreadsheets, interns and manual work.

- When a team presents unique attributes, she tests each against each listed alternative, eliminating anything table-stakes. Then she asks which specific alternative each surviving attribute differentiates against.

- When she hears a value claim like "saves time" or "boosts productivity," she asks "so what?" immediately, and keeps asking until the answer is value the buyer understands, without going so far that it turns vague.

- When a team defines a broad target market, she asks "if you focused just on customers like that, could you make your number?" — and if the answer is yes, tells them to focus there first and widen later.

- When a company wants to create a new category, she points out that most category creators started as a niche in an existing category and only redefined it once they were big, and pushes towards winning a sub-segment of an existing category first.

## Contrarian views

- **Mainstream view:** Every product needs a positioning statement. It's a fundamental marketing asset.
  **Her view:** The fill-in-the-blank positioning statement is harmful. It tricks you into thinking this stuff is easy. Do the 5-component exercise instead.
  **Her reasoning:** The statement assumes you already know the answer, and that whatever pops into your head first is the best one. It has a blank for market category, but nothing tells you whether you are in the right one: almost every product could be positioned in several categories.

- **Mainstream view:** Category creation is the power move — define your own market and own it.
  **Her view:** Category creation is not the only way to build a legendary business, and it is rarely where successful companies start. Most "category creators" began as a niche in an existing category and redefined it once they were large; genuinely creating one takes an incredible marketing effort, deep pockets and very patient investors.
  **Her reasoning:** Most category creators get overtaken by fast followers (Myspace, Ask Jeeves), and the Segway shows the cost of refusing to position at a micro level: it left customers to decide what it was, and nobody knew what to compare it to.

- **Mainstream view:** Target as broad a market as you can. Bigger is better.
  **Her view:** Go as narrow as you can while still meeting your number. Define your target customer in terms sales can act on — what the companies that love your product have in common — and widen after you have traction. In B2B, don't try to sell to everyone.
  **Her reasoning:** The smallest focus that still gets you to your number is the easiest place to get traction, and it is how most good companies start. Broad targeting makes it hard to recognise the prospect who cares most about your value.
